Measuring the wrong distance

School connectivity is judged by how quickly a test server answers. We measured how quickly the content answers, from inside the same networks. In 18 of 22 countries the two are not the same number — and the error runs both ways.

What was measured

Every traceroute study describes the path from a school to an M-Lab test server. Whether that path resembles the one a pupil actually uses depends on something the study cannot see: where the content they open is served from.

So we asked the second question directly. From 46 RIPE Atlas probes sitting inside the networks that carry school traffic, we measured the round trip to five destinations a school actually opens — Google Classroom, YouTube, Claude, Khan Academy and Wikipedia — and compared it with the M-Lab server latency for the same country.

When the server flatters

Eleven countries look worse on the test than they are in practice. Sri Lanka is the extreme: its schools are measured against servers in India at 175 ms, while the content they open answers in 31 ms. Claude resolves in three milliseconds there. Any finding built on that Indian path — including a long-running story about routing through Europe — describes a journey the schools are not making.

Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan are measured against servers in Pakistan, at 234 and 235 ms. Their content answers in 71 and 89. The server choice, not the country's connectivity, is producing most of that number.

When the server hides the problem

The opposite failure is quieter and more consequential. Seven countries — most of them the ones with a domestic test server — look better on the test than their pupils experience.

Benin is the clearest case. Its server sits in Lagos, one short hop away, and reports 12.7 ms. The content its schools open answers in 99 ms, nearly eight times further. Mongolia, Montenegro and Kenya all have servers inside their own borders, and all three report latencies two to five times better than the content their schools actually reach.

A domestic test server measures the last mile beautifully and says nothing about the journey that follows it. Where connectivity reporting is used to judge whether schools are adequately served, this is the direction that misleads.

Wikipedia is nobody's neighbour

One destination behaves unlike the rest. Wikipedia resolves to Amsterdam or Marseille for most of these countries — Singapore and Ashburn for a few — and answers in 37 to 193 ms. Botswana reaches Google in eight milliseconds and Wikipedia in 174. Lesotho, nine and 184. South Africa, five and 182.

Google, Anthropic and Fastly have moved their edges close to these schools. Wikimedia has not. A single national figure for “latency to content” conceals a gap that large, which is why the table above keeps the destinations apart.

What follows from this

  • A traceroute finding travels only as far as its server. Before a routing result is presented as something schools experience, check where the content in question is actually served.
  • Adding a test server changes the reported numbers. When M-Lab brought a Kochi server into service in July, 1,123 Sri Lankan schools improved by 9.8% on latency and 23.1% on throughput without anything changing at the schools.
  • Server placement is not neutral between operators. Servers hosted inside one ISP's network measure that ISP's customers on-net and everyone else across whatever interconnection exists.
  • Namibia is the case where the two agree. Its server sits in Cape Town, and Google Classroom, YouTube and Claude are all served from South Africa. A routing finding there does describe the path to the content.

Limits

Atlas probes sit in the same networks as schools, not in schools; a school's own last mile is not measured here. Probe coverage is uneven — Botswana, Lesotho, Rwanda, Benin, Malawi and Fiji had no probe inside a school-serving network, so any connected probe was used instead, and those rows describe the country's networks rather than its schools specifically. Sri Lanka's largest operator carries 55.6% of school traceroutes and is represented by one probe; Namibia's carries 93% and has none at all. Five destinations cannot stand for everything a school opens, and national learning platforms — often hosted domestically and reached in single-digit milliseconds — are excluded. One Uzbek probe returned roughly 3,000 ms on every target and was dropped.
