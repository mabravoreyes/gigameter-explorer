One Road Out

In two of twenty-one countries, a single company is the way out for more than four fifths of the schools whose route out can be seen at all — Uzbekistan, where it is the only company observed, and Benin, where eleven schools were measured. Only two thirds of the schools in this panel have a route that can be seen; every figure here is conditioned on that. Counting tests rather than schools put that figure at seven. These figures are computed on the school-filtered subset of M-Lab's traceroute data — not on the raw traffic across all M-Lab tests in a country, school and non-school.

This is a summary across the 21 countries where at least 200* school tests show the first network beyond the school’s own — an arbitrary threshold, chosen from a gap in the distribution of traces per country. Every figure describes traffic observed from M-Lab servers toward schools; routing is commonly asymmetric, so none of it need hold for traffic leaving a school.

Six countries fall below the threshold and are excluded: Kyrgyzstan (139 tests showing a next network), Grenada (89), Zambia (67), Saint Vincent and the Grenadines (54), Saint Lucia (3) and Congo (0).
*choosing the right threshold in future versions requires iteration.

The measure

Each traceroute runs from an M-Lab server toward a school. Walking back from the school, the first network beyond its own ISP is that test's egress operator — the network its provider hands to.

That handoff is frequently inside the country: Cogent takes Albanian school traffic from Abissnet in Tirana and carries it out via Belgrade, so egress marks a change of operator.

Concentration is the share of a country's school schools taking the same road, summarised by the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index, where 1.0 means one company is the way out for all of them.

Each school carries the same weight however often it tested, and that weight is split across the operators its own tests actually used. Without this a single heavy-measuring school can outvote a hundred others: Albania averages 105 tests per school, and counting tests rather than schools put Cogent at 82.4% instead of 53.2%. Shares are aggregated by company rather than by ASN, since one company can hold several — Kazakhtelekom does, and counting its ASNs separately made Kazakhstan look almost twice as competitive as it is.

Everything here is conditioned on being able to see a route

A traceroute that stops inside a school’s own network shows no next operator, so that school contributes nothing to any figure on this page. Across the panel that is 4,726 of 13,720 schools — Uzbekistan can see a route for 2,800 of its 6,167, Albania for 476 of 854, Botswana for 70 of 131.

Those schools are not missing at random: truncation follows the operator, so an ISP whose equipment does not answer traceroute disappears from its country’s figures entirely. Every share below is therefore a ceiling. If every unseen school used a different company, Uzbekistan’s 100% would be 45.4%, South Africa’s 78.3% would be 44.0%, and Albania’s 53.2% would be 29.7%.

Retail vs wholesale

Many sellers, one road

Two different markets sit behind a school's connection. The retail market is who sells to a school — measured here over distinct schools served. The wholesale market is who the school’s provider hands that traffic to. Both are shown as the reciprocal of HHI: the number of equally-sized providers the market behaves like.

The effective number of providers is a size-weighted count. It discounts each provider by how small its share is - in Albania for example, 19 companies are the way out for at least one school, but they weight to 3.03 because Cogent alone is the way out for 53% of them. 1/HHI gives a size-weighted count - how many equal-sized providers would produce the concentration actually observed.

HHI measures concentration, not redundancy

HHI is a function of shares alone. A country with 82% on one operator and eighteen others at 1% scores 0.674; one with 82% and a single operator at 18% scores 0.705, though only the second has a fallback large enough to absorb the load. The index was built to measure market power, where a fragmented tail does reduce one firm's dominance. It is not a redundancy measure, which is why the second operator's share is reported separately below.

Weighted by traffic, Albania’s 135 links resolve to 4.9

A topology graph draws an edge observed once exactly as it draws an edge observed ninety thousand times. The picture encodes which links exist; the index encodes how much they carry. Both are accurate, and they answer different questions.

Inside the border

The network the most school paths cross at home

Egress names the operator a school hands to on the way out. It says nothing about a network that every path crosses inside the country without being anyone’s egress. The published reports carry a chokepoint measure for this, taken over the whole path — which, applied to Albania, names Hrvatski Telekom at 91.6%, a network on the route from the measurement server in Podgorica rather than an Albanian dependency. Restricted to hops geolocating inside the country, and excluding each test’s own access network and the measurement host, the measure answers a narrower question: which single network inside our borders do the most school paths cross?

The denominator moves this number by up to 37×

Namibia reads 2.7% of all school tests, and 100% of the tests that have any in-country transit at all — 97.3% have none. Malawi reads 1.3% and 85.5% on the same split. Both readings are correct and they support opposite sentences, which is why the share with no in-country transit is shown as part of every bar rather than dropped from the denominator.

Why the filter matters

The raw exports are not schools

The published parquet is every NDT test in a country, not every school test. The school share ranges from 3% (Kenya) to 90% (Fiji), so for roughly half the panel an unfiltered figure describes the national consumer internet instead.

Malawi changes operator, not just magnitude

Unfiltered, Malawi's top egress reads as TENET, the South African research network, at HHI 0.428. On school traffic it is West Indian Ocean Cable Company at 76.5% of schools. Same country, same months, a different company — though 74% of those schools are on Starlink, and excluding satellite the figure falls to 39.7%.

Sri Lanka reconciles

The raw file gives HHI 0.237. School-filtered it is 0.487. The published country report gives 0.733, computed on the school subset and excluding tests with no observed egress — and Sri Lanka's school egress coverage is 46%. Population and denominator together account for the gap.

Limits

What these figures do not show

  • Egress coverage varies from 21% to 100%. A test whose traceroute stops before leaving the school's own network yields no egress at all. Where coverage is low the HHI rests on a minority of tests, and that minority is not random — it excludes operators whose paths truncate. Coverage is shown for every country in the table below.
  • Six countries' top operator is the measurement host — marked ⚑ throughout. South Africa and Botswana show TENET, Kenya shows KENET, Fiji shows REANNZ, Mongolia the Mongolian Technical University network, and Benin MTN Nigeria. Those are where M-Lab put the server, reached backwards. Dropping those hops does not fix it — it deletes precisely the operators that peer directly with the research network and keeps the ones that don't.
  • A third of the panel cannot support a point estimate. Intervals come from resampling schools, the unit of independence. Lesotho has five schools with an observable path and its interval runs from 8% to 82%; Trinidad, Honduras, Dominica, Rwanda, Kenya and Belize all span more than twenty points. Read those as ranges.
  • Satellite is a different question, and in four countries it dominates. Starlink is the access network for 74% of Malawi's measured schools, 60% of Lesotho's, 39% of Botswana's and 27% of Fiji's. Its egress reflects SpaceX's own peering — in Malawi, 91% hands to WIOCC in Johannesburg — not the country's routing. Excluding satellite schools moves Malawi from 76.5% to 39.7%, and Fiji the other way from 75.2% to 97.0%.
  • Every figure is a ceiling. Schools whose traceroutes stop before leaving their own network contribute nothing, and they are not missing at random — truncation follows the operator. Uzbekistan has 2,800 schools with an observable path and 3,367 without; if all of those used a different company its 100% would be 45.4%. South Africa's 78.3% has a floor of 44.0%, Albania's 53.2% a floor of 29.7%.
  • Six countries are excluded — for observability, not size. A country needs enough tests that reveal an egress to estimate a share distribution at all. Saint Vincent measures 19.4% of its connected schools — better coverage than Kazakhstan, Namibia or South Africa — but only 54 of its 1,896 traces reveal an egress. Saint Lucia: 3 of 461. Zambia: 67 of 6,717. Also excluded: Grenada (89), Kyrgyzstan (139) and Congo (0). The cut is 200 observed egresses, and nothing in the panel falls between 139 and 265 — any threshold in that range yields the same 21 countries. At 200 observations a share carries a 95% interval of roughly ±7 points at 50% and ±5 at 85%: adequate for whether one operator carries four fifths, not for separating two countries a few points apart. Country size is never a reason to exclude; several small states are among the best-covered panels in the set.

Method

Traceroutes are the published M-Lab country-month exports. School identity comes from joining the NDT test UUID (id) to Giga Meter (measurement_uuid), which attributes a test to a school rather than to an address — school IPs are dynamic, with a median of 18 addresses per school in Fiji and single addresses carrying up to 14 schools.

Egress operator is the first AS beyond the school's own network on the collapsed AS path, equivalent to the "first observed transit" view in the published reports. Shares are computed per school, not per test: each school contributes weight 1.0, divided among the operators its own tests used, so measurement volume cannot drive a country's result. ASNs are aggregated to companies by normalised organisation name. Intervals are 95% percentile bootstraps over 400 resamples of schools. The other two views those reports carry — first AS outside the country, and first foreign-registered AS — need AS registration country, which the exports do not include. HHI is computed over tests that yield an egress; tests without one are reported separately as coverage rather than folded into the denominator.
