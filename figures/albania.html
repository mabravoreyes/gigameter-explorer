Giga Meter · traceroute study

Three routes out of Albania

Albanian schools reach the same test server 130 km away by three different paths, and which one a school gets is decided by its internet provider — not by where the school is. The slowest is fourteen times the latency of the fastest, and every other measure is worse on it too.

Everything here is restricted to traceroutes attributable to a Giga-registered school by NDT test ID — 94,315 of the 140,915 in the Albanian export. The other third are non-school clients on the same networks.

The finding

Same country, same destination, 14× apart

Every school below is reaching one M-Lab server in Podgorica. The only thing that differs is the path its traffic takes to get there.

The direct path is not hypothetical — 19 schools already use it. The throughput differences between routes are largely which operators take which route; the section below separates the two.

The mechanism

The route follows the provider

Four small operators peer directly into Montenegro and deliver 4 ms. The incumbent, Telekom Albania, sends 96.9% of its school traffic through Italy, Germany or Austria. Albania's regional interconnection is being done by its smallest providers.

How each operator routes its school traffic

Each school's own route split, averaged across that operator's schools — so one school with 1,871 tests counts the same as one with five, and a school that uses two routes still shows both. Latency is the median of per-school medians. Schools need five completed traces to count; operators need 250.

Counting every trace instead barely moves the two largest — Abissnet 91.9% via Serbia against 91.3%, Telekom Albania 96.6% via Europe against 97.7%. It moves the small ones: Albtelecom's European share reads 16.6% by trace and 26.9% by school, and Tele.Co.Albania 64.4% via Serbia against 39.1%. Their heaviest testers are not typical of their estates.

The same school, routed two ways, is 37 ms slower on the long path. 129 Abissnet schools have enough traces on both upstreams to compare against themselves: median 26 ms via Cogent against 64 ms via Arelion, and 128 of the 129 are slower on Arelion (Wilcoxon p = 6×10⁻²³). The school, its access network, its contract and its destination are all held constant. Only the route changes.

Robustness

The route costs latency, not bandwidth

Every measurement here is NDT7, with download, upload, latency and loss recorded. Holding the operator fixed separates what the route causes from what the operator does — Abissnet carries 43,000 traces across two different upstreams to the same server.

One network, two upstreams, same destination

Abissnet's completed traceroutes to Podgorica, split by the transit provider they hand off to. Same access network by construction — and largely the same schools: 183 of 226 appear on both upstreams, with only one school exclusive to Arelion. The ribbon carries the countries each path crosses.

Ribbon width follows trace volume, with the thinner path widened to stay legible — Arelion carries 7.7% of school traces but reaches 184 of Abissnet's 226 schools, so most of the network's schools are seen on the long path at some point.

What the longer route costs the same school

The 129 Abissnet schools with enough traces on both upstreams, each compared against itself. Bars are the median within-school change as a percentage — always drawn as a penalty, whichever way the underlying number moves. Every measure is worse on the longer route; latency is worse by far.

Every measure is significantly worse on the longer route, so this is not a latency-only effect — but the magnitudes are not comparable. Latency rises 142% while download falls about 3%. A school on the long path loses a couple of Mb/s and gains 37 ms. That matters because most school internet use — a page loading, a video call, a login — is bound by round trips rather than by sustained bandwidth.

Latency is the effect worth arguing about, not the only one. Download and upload are both significantly worse on the long route, by roughly 3%. Latency is worse by 142%. Frame the case on latency because that is where the size is — and because round trips, not bandwidth, are what a page load, a video call or a login actually waits on.

Who pays for it

Two different problems, often confused

The regional pattern is routing: Dibër is closer to Podgorica than Tirana is, and it is slow because 89% of its traffic goes through western Europe. The rural–urban gap is not. Rural schools trail on download and upload but barely on latency — that is access capacity, and peering will not fix it.

The trajectory

It improved to May, then gave much of it back

The Giga Meter campaign began in March 2026 and the fleet grew from 99 schools to 856 by May, so a country average would move on composition alone. This tracks only the 67 schools present in every month from April to July — the same schools throughout.

On the June-to-July pair only 32% of these schools improved on latency. The reversal coincides with the end of the school year, but this is the same panel throughout.

Only latency reversed

The same 67 schools on all four NDT7 measures, each indexed to its April value. Download is at its best in July, in the month latency is worst.

The schools did not get worse — their routing did. "The gains reversed" is wrong; "the latency gains reversed while capacity kept improving" is right, and is a cleaner argument for treating routing as its own policy object. Retransmission is shown for completeness only: the field labelled packet loss is s2c_bytes_retrans ÷ s2c_bytes_sent, a TCP retransmission rate under a test that deliberately saturates the link — not loss on the path. Don't build a claim on it.

The one trend that did not reverse: the direct Albania→Montenegro path did not exist in March and carried 4.3% of attributed school paths by July. New regional interconnection is appearing, and it is measurable.

Share of schools whose main route is the direct one

One school, one vote, among schools with at least five completed traces that month. Counted by traces instead it reads lower — 4.3% in July — because the heaviest-testing schools are not the ones on the direct path.

What would undercut this

  • One vantage point. 96% of traces end at Podgorica. These are routes to that server, not to the internet in general — the direct path's 4 ms says Montenegro is close, not that those schools are fast to everything.
  • The panel is small early. March had 99 schools measuring. A March-anchored comparison rests on 84 schools and is also the most flattering endpoint available; April-to-June (253 schools, −37%) is the load-bearing version.
  • Client location is not verified. The median test sat 0.21 km from its registered school, but 52 km at the 90th percentile, with 1,015 tests beyond 10 km.
  • "Packet loss" is not packet loss. The field is s2c_bytes_retrans ÷ s2c_bytes_sent — a retransmission rate under a saturating transfer. Albania's median reads 3.83%, which would cripple a link if it were real loss. It carries no quality claim here.
  • Counts run above the published country pages. The school filter is applied downstream of the source files; the join used here lands about 17% high.

Unlike most findings in this dataset, this one can be checked independently: 82.4% of Albanian school traffic sits in a network with a live RIPE Atlas probe, so the routes can be re-tested toward destinations schools actually use.
