Giga · M-Lab traceroute studies

Where school content actually lives

Across 22 countries, the M-Lab test server used to measure school connectivity sits outside the range of latencies schools actually experience in 9 of them. Content is not at one distance: national learning platforms answer in 1 ms or 320 ms depending on where a ministry hosts them, AI services answer in under 25 ms almost everywhere, and Wikipedia answers from Europe in nearly every country measured. We measured all of it from RIPE Atlas probes inside the networks that carry school traffic.

Content sits at three distances, not one

Giga's traceroute studies measure the path from a school to an M-Lab server. What a school opens is somewhere else, and not in one place. We grouped destinations into three layers and measured each from the same probes: the national learning platform, global services (Google Classroom, Google Search, YouTube, Khan Academy, Wikipedia) and AI services (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini).

The spread within a single country is often larger than the spread between countries. Botswana reaches global services in 8 ms and Wikipedia in 174. Sri Lanka reaches AI services in 4 ms and its M-Lab server in 175.

A ministry's own platform is often the furthest thing away

Of the 17 national learning platforms measured, eight are hosted domestically — on the incumbent carrier or the national research network — and answer in 1 to 24 ms. Three sit behind Cloudflare or Google Cloud at 12 to 38 ms. Six are hosted on foreign clouds, and for those countries the ministry's own platform is the slowest destination its schools reach.

South Africa's Department of Basic Education cloud answers in 255 ms from South African school networks. From the same probes, Claude answers in 5 ms and global services in 27. Zambia's platform is on AWS in Oregon at 320 ms; Malawi's on a German host at 196; Kenya's on Microsoft at 139; Benin's on a German host at 135.

A domestic test server measures the access link accurately and reports nothing about the path beyond it. In every country above with a foreign-hosted platform, the M-Lab server is domestic or regional — so the reported connectivity figure is good while the platform the ministry built is five to twenty times further away.

Wikipedia is the outlier everywhere

Google, Anthropic, OpenAI and Fastly have edge capacity close to these schools. Wikimedia does not. In 21 of 22 countries Wikipedia resolves to Amsterdam, Marseille, Singapore or Ashburn, and it is the slowest of the global services in almost every country measured — frequently by an order of magnitude over what the same school reaches Google in.

Which networks carry the schools, and which can be measured

School traffic is not spread evenly across a country's operators. In Namibia one network carries 93% of school traceroutes; in Sri Lanka two carry 85%. Whether a finding about a country is verifiable depends on whether RIPE Atlas has a probe inside those specific networks — and often it does not.

The route out is not the route back

Giga's traceroute exports record the path from the M-Lab server towards the school. We measured the opposite direction — school network to server — from probes inside 18 of the same networks, and compared the transit path excluding the origin.

Seven of the 18 traverse an identical sequence of networks both ways. Eleven do not. Sri Lanka's two largest operators both differ: Sri Lanka Telecom shares 40% of its transit between directions and Dialog 25%, meaning findings drawn from the inbound path there describe a route outbound traffic does not take.

What this bears on

  • A traceroute finding travels only as far as its server. Before a routing result is presented as something schools experience, establish where the content in question is hosted.
  • Adding a test server moves the reported numbers. When M-Lab brought a Kochi server into service in July 2026, 1,123 Sri Lankan schools improved 9.8% on latency and 23.1% on throughput with no change at the schools.
  • Server placement is not neutral between operators. A server hosted inside one ISP measures that ISP's customers on-net and everyone else across whatever interconnection exists.
  • Where footprint would change what is measured: Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan are measured through Pakistan at 226–235 ms while their content sits at 70–95. Those three countries account for roughly 10,300 measuring schools.

Method and limits

Measurements ran 30–31 August 2026 from 46 RIPE Atlas probes across 22 countries, selected inside the autonomous systems carrying school traceroutes where such a probe exists. M-Lab figures are median completed-path RTT from the Giga traceroute exports, February–July 2026.

Probes sit in school networks, not in schools; a school's own last mile is not measured. Six countries — Botswana, Lesotho, Rwanda, Benin, Malawi and Fiji — had no probe inside a school-serving network, so any connected probe was used and those rows describe the country's networks rather than its schools. Namibia's largest operator carries 93% of school traceroutes and has no live probe at all. Five national platforms could not be measured for want of a probe, and two did not resolve. Hosts that do not answer ICMP are reported at the last responding hop and are lower bounds. One Uzbek probe returned approximately 3,000 ms on every destination and was excluded.
