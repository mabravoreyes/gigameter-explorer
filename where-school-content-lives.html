Where school content is hosted

In 9 of 22 countries, the M-Lab test server used to measure school connectivity has a round trip outside the range of every destination schools actually reach. Destination latency varies by content type: national learning platforms answer in 1 ms or 320 ms depending on where a ministry hosts them, AI services answer in under 25 ms in almost every country, and Wikipedia answers from Europe or North America in 21 of 22. All destination measurements were taken from RIPE Atlas probes inside the networks carrying school traffic.

Draft v0 — Methodology Note

Every active measurement on this page is a single one-off traceroute per probe per destination, taken on 30–31 August 2026. Internet paths change over hours and days, under load, and with routing policy. One sample can capture a transient route as easily as a stable one. This artifact is to demonstrate the potential of this research rather than to draw conclusions

The two sides of every comparison are not sampled to the same depth. The M-Lab figures aggregate six months and, in the larger countries, tens of thousands of measurements. The Atlas figures beside them come from one to three probes taking one measurement each..

This affects the forward-path and asymmetry section most directly: the claim that seven of eighteen school networks route identically in both directions rests on one outbound trace per network. It should be read as an indication that asymmetry is common, not as a measurement of how common.

What would make this robust: repeated measurements at intervals over several days per probe, more probes per network where they exist, and periodic re-runs so a path can be seen to hold or change. Directional findings are likely to survive that.

Destination latency by content tier

Giga's traceroute studies measure the path between a school and an M-Lab server. The destinations schools open are hosted elsewhere, and at a range of distances. Destinations were grouped into three tiers and each measured from the same probes: the national learning platform, global services (Google Classroom, Google Search, YouTube, Khan Academy, Wikipedia) and AI services (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini).

The spread within a single country is often larger than the spread between countries. Botswana reaches global services in 8 ms and Wikipedia in 174. Sri Lanka reaches AI services in 4 ms and its M-Lab server in 175.

Where national learning platforms are hosted

Of the 17 national learning platforms measured, eight are hosted domestically — on the incumbent carrier or the national research network — and answer in 1 to 24 ms. Three sit behind Cloudflare or Google Cloud at 12 to 38 ms. Six are hosted on foreign clouds. In those countries the ministry's own platform is the slowest destination measured.

South Africa's Department of Basic Education cloud answers in 255 ms from South African school networks. From the same probes, Claude answers in 5 ms and global services in 27. Zambia's platform is on AWS in Oregon at 320 ms; Malawi's on a German host at 196; Kenya's on Microsoft at 139; Benin's on a German host at 135.

Wikipedia against the other global services

Google, Anthropic, OpenAI and Fastly serve these countries from edge capacity close to the school networks measured. Wikimedia does not. In 21 of 22 countries Wikipedia resolved to Amsterdam, Marseille, Singapore or Ashburn, and it is the slowest global service measured in almost every country — in several cases by an order of magnitude relative to Google from the same probe.

School-serving networks and probe coverage

School traffic is not spread evenly across a country's operators. In Namibia one network carries 93% of school traceroutes; in Sri Lanka two carry 85%. Independent verification of a country-level finding depends on RIPE Atlas holding a probe inside those specific networks. In many cases it does not.

Forward and reverse paths

Giga's traceroute exports record the path from the M-Lab server towards the school. We measured the opposite direction, school network to server, from probes inside 18 of the same networks, and compared the transit path excluding the origin.

Seven of the 18 traverse an identical sequence of networks both ways. Eleven do not. Sri Lanka's two largest operators both differ: Sri Lanka Telecom shares 40% of its transit networks between directions and Dialog 25%. Findings drawn from the inbound path in those networks do not necessarily describe the outbound path.

Implications for traceroute studies

  • A traceroute result describes the path to the server it measured. Where such a result is presented as representative of what schools experience, the hosting location of the relevant content should be established first.
  • Adding a test server moves the reported numbers. When M-Lab brought a Kochi server into service in July 2026, 1,123 Sri Lankan schools improved 9.8% on latency and 23.1% on throughput with no corresponding change at the schools.
  • Server placement is not neutral between operators. A server hosted inside one ISP measures that ISP's own customers on-net, and every other operator's customers across whatever interconnection exists between them.
  • Where footprint would change what is measured: Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan are measured through Pakistan at 226–235 ms while their content sits at 70–95. Those three countries account for roughly 10,300 measuring schools.

Method and limits

Measurements ran 30–31 August 2026 from 46 RIPE Atlas probes across 22 countries, selected inside the autonomous systems carrying school traceroutes where such a probe exists. M-Lab figures are median completed-path RTT from the Giga traceroute exports, February–July 2026.

Probes sit in school networks, not in schools; a school's own last mile is not measured. Six countries — Botswana, Lesotho, Rwanda, Benin, Malawi and Fiji — had no probe inside a school-serving network, so any connected probe was used and those rows describe the country's networks rather than its schools. Namibia's largest operator carries 93% of school traceroutes and has no live probe at all. Five national platforms could not be measured for want of a probe, and two did not resolve. Hosts that do not answer ICMP are reported at the last responding hop and are lower bounds. One Uzbek probe returned approximately 3,000 ms on every destination and was excluded.

Annex — transit and exchange point exposure

Two operators come up repeatedly when school routing is discussed: Arelion (AS1299, formerly Telia Carrier) as a transit carrier, and Equinix as an interconnection venue. They appear in this dataset in different roles.

Arelion appears on a third of school networks

Of 222 school-serving networks with at least 50 measured paths, 72 — 32% — cross Arelion at some point between the school and the M-Lab server. It appears in 12 of 25 countries. Moldova is the most exposed, with Arelion on 45.9% of all school paths; Honduras follows at 21.7%, Belize at 14.5% and Albania at 13.5%.

Exposure is not evenly spread inside a country. In Albania, Telekom Albania routes 95.2% of its school traffic through Arelion while Abissnet, the largest network, routes almost none. Three smaller Albanian networks and one South African satellite operator cross Arelion on 100% of measured paths.

Equinix appears as an exchange, not as a carrier

Equinix is almost absent as a transit network: only two school networks cross an Equinix autonomous system, across 91 hops in the entire dataset. As an interconnection point it appears more often: school traffic in seven countries crosses an Equinix exchange.

Fiji has the highest exchange exposure measured: 88.6% of its school paths traverse Equinix Sydney, the most-crossed exchange in this dataset. Sri Lanka crosses an Equinix exchange on 7.3% of paths, Kazakhstan 6.8% via Equinix Singapore, Belize 5.7% and Honduras 2.0% via Equinix Los Angeles.

Regional exchanges carry more school traffic than either

School traffic in most of these countries does cross an exchange, and usually a regional one. Rwanda routes 95.3% of school paths through KIXP in Nairobi and Kenya 84.5%; Namibia crosses NAPAfrica Cape Town on 71.4% and Lesotho NAPAfrica Johannesburg on 66.6%; Uzbekistan reaches DE-CIX Frankfurt on 64.4%.

Albania is the exception. Its most-used exchange, SOX in Serbia, appears on 0.1% of paths. An earlier reading of the Albanian and Belizean data alone indicated that school traffic rarely crosses exchange points; across all 25 countries that does not hold, and Albania is unusual rather than typical.

Method: an autonomous system is counted as Arelion where the hop's ASN is 1299 or its organisation name matches Arelion or Telia. Exchange crossings use the IXP prefix annotation carried in the traceroute exports, so an exchange whose prefixes are not in that dataset will not appear. Counts are per path — a path crossing an exchange twice counts once.
