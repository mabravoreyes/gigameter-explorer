Giga Meter · M-Lab traceroute study

The Same Road, Twice

The published traceroute exports carry every NDT client in a country, not only schools. Splitting them by whether a test is known to the Giga Meter backend, and comparing the two groups inside the same access network, the same measurement server and the same hour, isolates what is left when the route is held fixed.

Draft v0 23 countries · February–July 2026 Analysis: helpers/pairwise_arms.py
What is being compared

Two groups, not two premises

Every traceroute row carries id, the NDT test UUID, which Giga Meter measurements also carry as measurement_uuid. Joining on it splits each country's export in two: traces the Meter backend can attribute to a registered school, and traces it has no record of.

Read this before any number below

The second group is unattributed, not verified non-school. It holds household and business clients on the same networks, but it also holds school tests the backend never recorded. Every figure here is a contrast in attribution, not in premises — and the last chart in this page measures how badly that bites per country.

A cell is one access network, one M-Lab server, one three-hour daypart and weekday-or-weekend. Cells are kept only where both groups carry at least 30 traces, so no figure rests on a cell one group occupies alone.

The confound

Without the clock held fixed, this measures time of day

The two groups are sampled from different clocks in every country. In Albania 22% of school traces land in the 08:00 hour against 4% of unattributed ones, which sit in the residential evening peak; school traces are 92% weekday against 75%. Across the 23 countries the hour-of-day distributions differ by a total variation distance of 0.17 to 0.61.

After the control

There is no fleet-wide answer

With the network, the server and the clock all held fixed, the countries split. School traces are slower in some and faster in others, and the split does not follow region, volume or school count. This is a per-country question.

How clean is the contrast

In three countries the comparison is schools against schools

An unattributed trace from a client IP that also carries attributed traces is very likely a school test the backend never recorded. The share of the unattributed group in that position bounds how contaminated the comparison is.

Namibia

One operator, and it is not the route

Namibia's +63 ms is not spread across the country. It belongs to a single operator, and the path check rules out routing as the cause.

Both groups take the same AS path — AS2018 → AS20459 → AS36996 — as their dominant route, in 84.5% and 89.1% of traces respectively, and the most-travelled path is identical in all 29 comparable Namibian cells. Latency up to the hand-off into Telecom Namibia's network is 18.3 ms for school traces and 18.2 ms for unattributed ones. The route and everything on the way to it are the same.

Inference — not yet verified

In the largest weekday cell the idle path latency gap is −2.1 ms while the loaded gap is +122.0 ms. Same route, same border latency, no difference when the link is quiet, and a large one while a transfer is running: that shape is queueing on the school access link rather than a longer or worse path. Routing would not care that it is Saturday. This is consistent with congestion or an aggregation point Telecom Namibia puts school circuits behind, but the traceroute data cannot distinguish those two, and the jnb02 and cpt01 cells show part of the gap even at idle — so the access link is not the whole story.

Limits

What this is not

Direction of measurement. Every path runs from the M-Lab server towards the client. Routing is often asymmetric, so none of this need hold for traffic leaving a school.

Loss is absent on purpose. The exports carry ndt_loss_rate, but it is a retransmission rate and retransmission rises with throughput — the group that transfers faster mechanically shows more of it. It is excluded from the default metrics rather than reported with a caveat.

Thin school arms. Kenya's effect is significant but its school group is 3.0% of traces; South Africa's is 12.8%. Read those two against the trace counts in the table.

Not the published filter. The country reports filter to curated school IP ranges, which are not in these files. This join yields school identity instead, which is what the reports' own School-Level Study uses, but the two populations are not identical.
