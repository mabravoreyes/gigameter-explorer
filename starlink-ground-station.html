Starlink in school networks

Schools in 18 of the 37 countries Giga Meter covers now measure on Starlink. We compared its routing and performance against the terrestrial operators serving schools in the same countries. Ground station placement accounts for most of the variation, and in Malawi — the only country with enough schools on both links to control for placement — the throughput advantage largely disappears.

Coverage

Starlink serves schools in 18 countries. Malawi has the highest share of measuring schools at 63%, up from 20% in June 2025. Kazakhstan, Botswana and Fiji sit between 15% and 19% and have been flat since late 2025. Mongolia is at 4%.

Malawi’s fleet is a Giga programme deployment rather than market adoption: schools were selected and connected. Its estate is 75% rural against 53% for the terrestrial estate in the same country.

Ground station placement

Starlink traffic leaves the dish, returns to earth at a ground station, and joins the terrestrial internet there. We identified the ground station from the first non-Starlink hop in each traceroute, walking outward from the school.

Two of six countries hand off inside their own borders: Kenya in Nairobi, Kazakhstan in Almaty. The rest hand off abroad — Malawi and Botswana in Johannesburg, Mongolia in Tokyo, Fiji in Sydney.

Latency follows placement. Mongolia has a test server in Ulaanbaatar that terrestrial operators reach in 8.7 ms. Starlink traffic from the same schools reaches Tokyo, hands to a Japanese carrier, and returns: 111 ms to a destination in its own capital. Botswana’s traffic to South Africa transits the United States on 75% of paths.

Within-school comparison

Compared across schools, Malawi’s Starlink schools deliver a median 32.3 Mb/s against 5.6 Mb/s for terrestrial schools. That comparison includes the placement effect: the programme was deployed to the worst-served schools.

Eighteen Malawian schools recorded at least 20 tests on each link. Comparing within school removes the placement effect. On that basis the median gain is 0.1 Mb/s, 9 of 18 schools are faster on Starlink, and the difference is not significant (Wilcoxon p = 0.23). Latency reverses: Starlink is a median 29 ms slower within school, and lower only at 6 of 18.

Starlink performance at these schools is bimodal. Eight schools record 15 Mb/s or more, median 29.8. Nine record under 5 Mb/s, median 1.6. Few sit between.

Which group a school falls into does not correlate with the speed of the line it replaced (ρ = +0.11, p = 0.66). The variation is a property of the Starlink installation — obstruction, service tier, terminal model, or a dish shared across a site. Eighteen schools are not enough to separate those; installation records would.

Throughput and packet loss

Starlink delivers 2–6× the throughput of terrestrial operators in most of these countries, measured across schools. Packet loss runs higher in every country except Botswana, by up to two orders of magnitude.

Loss at these rates degrades interactive traffic — video conferencing, real-time collaboration — while leaving bulk download largely unaffected. Contracts specified in megabits per second do not capture it.

Limitations

  • No cost data. Comparisons here are technical only.
  • Only Malawi has enough schools on both links to control for placement. Elsewhere the comparison is between different schools; Starlink schools are systematically more remote, which would understate rather than inflate its advantage.
  • Observational comparison between operators over the same period, not an experiment. Terrestrial performance may have changed independently.
  • Packet loss is a period median. Whether it rises during teaching hours is a separate measurement.
  • The hand-off hop indicates where traffic enters the terrestrial internet, which is consistent with a ground station in that city and with a nearby station landing into the same exchange.
  • Hop geolocation is imperfect and can place a domestic operator’s routers abroad.
